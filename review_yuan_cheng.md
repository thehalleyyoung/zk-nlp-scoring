# Review: Spectacles: A Verified Compiler from Semiring-Weighted Automata to Zero-Knowledge Scoring Circuits

**Reviewer:** Yuan Cheng (Probabilistic Modeling and Stochastic Processes)
**Score: 8/10**

---

The algebraic decomposition of NLP scoring metrics into weighted finite automata over explicitly chosen semirings is a remarkably clean abstraction that brings genuine mathematical structure to what is typically ad-hoc Python code. From a probabilistic modeling perspective, the choice of the counting semiring (ℕ, +, ·) for n-gram precision and the Boolean semiring ({0,1}, ∨, ∧) for exact match is mathematically natural and well-justified. The formal-power-series semantics provides an unambiguous denotation for each metric—something the evaluation community has lacked. The two-tier compilation architecture is intellectually honest: rather than papering over the fact that the tropical semiring (ℤ ∪ {+∞}, min, +) does not embed into a prime field via a semiring homomorphism, the authors introduce gadget-assisted compilation with a separate mechanized soundness proof (`circuit_sound_tropical`). This distinction between embeddable and non-embeddable semirings is precisely the kind of mathematical precision that elevates this work above engineering-only contributions. The BLEU-4 decomposition into four independent n-gram precision proofs—each over a ~100-state WFA rather than a monolithic O(V⁴)-state automaton—is a clever application of algebraic independence that makes the system tractable. The differential testing regime (100K random pairs across reference, WFA, and circuit implementations, with fixed-point precision ε = 2⁻²⁴) is thorough and appropriate for validating cross-representation agreement.

However, the probabilistic aspects of the contamination detection component deserve scrutiny. The OPRF-based PSI protocol for n-gram overlap detection operates under a semi-honest security model, which is a significant limitation for the adversarial setting the system purports to address. A model provider willing to commit contamination fraud is, by definition, not semi-honest. The claimed 50–100× communication reduction from trie-structured PSI exploiting prefix sharing is plausible for natural-language 8-grams but the estimate relies on "natural-language prefix-sharing statistics" without rigorous distributional analysis. For adversarial inputs or morphologically complex languages (e.g., Turkish, Finnish), prefix sharing could be dramatically lower, reducing the practical advantage to perhaps 5–10×. The contamination threshold τ is treated as a fixed parameter, but the choice of τ has profound implications for Type I/II error rates that are not analyzed. What constitutes an acceptable n-gram overlap between training and test data is itself a distributional question—common n-grams from the language's background distribution will create baseline overlap that varies across languages and domains. The contamination sensitivity target (≥ 0.99 detection rate at τ = 0.01 with true overlap 0.02) and specificity target (≤ 0.01 false alarm at overlap = 0) assume clean separation that may not hold for realistic benchmark-training distributions where incidental overlap is non-negligible.

The pass@k metric inclusion is well-motivated but raises distributional concerns that the proposal does not address. Pass@k involves sampling k programs and computing the fraction that pass test cases—this is fundamentally a Bernoulli trial repeated k times, and the WFA representation treats it as "repeated exact match with counting aggregation." This is semantically correct but obscures the statistical estimation aspect: the variance of pass@k estimates depends on the number of samples and the true pass rate, and the ZK proof certifies the computation on a fixed set of samples without addressing the statistical uncertainty in the metric itself. A STARK proof that pass@10 = 0.3 on a specific set of samples says nothing about whether this estimate is statistically meaningful. This is not a flaw in the system's claims (which are carefully scoped to computational correctness), but it represents a gap between what the certificate proves and what users likely care about. The 117K–142K total LoC estimate with 94K–114K novel LoC is ambitious but the subsystem breakdown is credible, particularly the 12K–15K Lean 4 component with its explicit sorry management discipline. The estimated proof generation latency of 1–30 seconds per metric on an Apple M2 is plausible given the stated WFA state counts (≤ 1,100 states) and AIR trace widths.
