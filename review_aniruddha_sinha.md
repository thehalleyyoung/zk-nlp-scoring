# Review: Spectacles: A Verified Compiler from Semiring-Weighted Automata to Zero-Knowledge Scoring Circuits

**Reviewer:** Aniruddha Sinha (Model Checking and AI Verification)
**Score: 7/10**

---

The TLA+ protocol specification component of Spectacles is well-scoped: 6 safety invariants and 2 liveness properties for the commit-reveal-verify protocol, checked via TLC model checking. This is appropriate for verifying the protocol's state machine against properties like commitment binding and abort recovery. However, the proposal allocates only 6K–8K LoC to the TLA+ specification (including Java/Rust infrastructure), which is modest for a protocol with three adversary capabilities (A1–A3) and the complex interaction between STARK verification and PSI contamination checking. The explicit limitation regarding adversary capability A2 (model identity substitution) is commendable—too many papers in this space claim completeness they cannot deliver. The acknowledgment that "Spectacles certifies computation on committed outputs, not the identity of the model that produced those outputs" is precisely the kind of honest scoping that strengthens trust in the claims that are made. The Fiat-Shamir transcript construction with domain separation and the abort-recovery state machine are standard but non-trivial protocol engineering that benefit from TLA+ modeling.

From a model checking perspective, the verification architecture has a significant structural gap: the soundness of the overall system depends on the composition of multiple independently verified components (STARK proofs, PSI protocol, commitment scheme), but the composition itself is verified only via "standard sequential composition (Canetti 2001, Theorem 4)" without a mechanized proof of the composition's correctness. The proposal acknowledges that "a full UC treatment of the composition is future work," but this is precisely where model checking could provide the most value. The state space of the composed protocol—where a cheating prover might interleave STARK proof generation with PSI queries in adversarial ways—is exactly the kind of concurrent protocol interaction that model checking excels at exploring. The 10K cheating-prover soundness tests are valuable but are inherently incomplete: they test specific adversarial strategies, not all possible interleavings. A TLA+ specification of the composed protocol (not just the commit-reveal-verify sub-protocol) would significantly strengthen the contribution. Additionally, the `kleene_dec` tactic in the Lean 4 library uses `native_decide` calls bounded to WFAs with ≤ 20 states "to prevent compilation blowup," with larger instances using `sorry`-with-documentation. From a verification completeness standpoint, this creates a boundary where the mechanized guarantees end and trust assumptions begin—exactly the kind of boundary that should be explicitly explored via model checking of the tactic's behavior at the boundary.

The evaluation plan for specification equivalence—comparing `wfa_equiv` tactic verdicts against brute-force enumeration for all strings up to length 20 on 1K random WFA pairs with ≤ 10 states—is well-designed as a bounded verification strategy but reveals the fundamental tension between decidability and practical verification. The Hopcroft minimization and coalgebraic bisimulation approach is theoretically complete for WFA equivalence over commutative semirings, but the mechanized proof of correctness is classified as "should-prove" rather than "must-prove." This means the equivalence decision procedure—one of the three headline contributions—may ship with `sorry` annotations in its correctness proof. For a system whose value proposition centers on eliminating trust in unverified metric implementations, having an unverified verifier is a non-trivial concern. The zero-knowledge testing approach (χ² statistical indistinguishability test with p > 0.05 over 10K samples) is standard but the threshold choice deserves justification: with 10K samples, the test has substantial power to detect small distribution differences, but whether the simulation accurately captures the real protocol's distribution is itself an unverified assumption. The end-to-end performance target of < 60 minutes for 50 examples across all seven metrics is realistic given the per-metric timing estimates, and the CPU-only execution constraint is well-motivated by the choice of STARKs over SNARKs.
